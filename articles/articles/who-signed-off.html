Who Signed Off?

When your AI made 10,000 decisions overnight and the auditor walks in Monday

Compliance Problem
→
Architecture Problem

Monday, February 3, 2025. A chief legal officer at a mid-size European fintech walks onto the engineering floor and asks a question nobody in the room can answer: "Which of our production AI systems use social scoring?"

Not "do we use social scoring?" — which of them do. Per endpoint. With documented evidence.

The night before, the EU AI Act's Article 5 prohibitions had taken effect. February 2, 2025 — six months after the regulation entered force on August 1, 2024. No phased rollout. No grace period. The bans applied to every AI system already running in production, already making decisions at volume. Social scoring. Real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces. Predictive policing. Emotion recognition in workplaces and schools. Manipulation of vulnerable groups. All prohibited — with penalties up to €35,000,000 or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

For a company with €10 billion in revenue, maximum exposure: €700 million. For €50 billion: €3.5 billion. These aren't projections — they're in Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 99, Paragraph 3.

The CLO isn't asking because she read the regulation last week. She's asking because the board read it this morning, and they want exposure quantified by close of business.

Nobody can answer. Not because the answer is complicated. Because the question requires infrastructure that doesn't exist.

The Bill Nobody Built

The cost anatomy. This fintech processes 15,000 loan applications daily. Each routes through four model endpoints — credit scoring, fraud detection, document parsing, risk categorization. That's 60,000 model-invocation events every 24 hours, distributed across roughly 20 microservices.

Before February 2, zero of those 60,000 invocation events carried a prohibited-practice attestation. Not because anyone was negligent. Because "does this inference pathway use social scoring?" was never part of the inference pipeline. It lived in the model card — a document written at training time, reviewed once, filed in Confluence.

A compliance analyst reviewing one decision pathway — tracing features, checking model documentation, verifying no prohibited signals leak in — needs roughly 45 minutes. Sixty thousand pathways × 45 minutes = 2.7 million analyst-minutes per day. Divided by one working day (480 minutes), that requires 5,625 full-time compliance analysts. Just to keep pace with a single day of decisions.

[BEAT 1] Perspective Shift

Legal will review model outputs after deployment.

Legal review doesn't scale to 60,000 calls/day. Compliance attestation must run in the inference pipeline — as middleware, not a quarterly spreadsheet.

The math was never going to work. Manual review is O(n) in decision volume; production AI volume grows monthly. You can't staff your way out of an O(n) audit requirement when n doubles every quarter.

Surfaces, Not Volume

Every microservice you add to serve a new model feature doesn't just add capacity — it adds a compliance surface that must be attested independently. Fifteen microservices each calling three models: 45 compliance surfaces. Twenty microservices each calling four: 80. The audit burden scales with the product of service count and model count, not with decision volume alone.

[BEAT 2] Perspective Shift

We'll add compliance checks after shipping the new microservice.

The 21st microservice doesn't add 1 compliance surface — it adds 4. Each needs its own prohibited-practice attestation before taking its first production request.

The audit gap isn't a compliance problem — it's an architecture problem. You cannot retroactively inspect what you didn't instrument at the point of decision. The regulation penalizes the absence of demonstrable compliance, and demonstration requires infrastructure that must exist in the inference path, not in a post-hoc spreadsheet. Your service mesh is your compliance mesh, whether you designed it that way or not.

The Deployer Owns It

In December 2024, the European Commission published the second draft of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, covering providers of foundation models classified as systemic risk — those trained with cumulative compute exceeding 10^25 FLOPS. The code requires providers to maintain technical documentation, conduct copyright compliance assessments, and evaluate systemic risks.

One detail the draft makes explicit, which many deployers overlook: the deployer's obligation is independent of the provider's. Using a compliant model in a non-compliant way still violates Article 5. The model card says "not designed for social scoring." Your feature pipeline feeds in zip code and employment gap anyway. The model uses zip code as a proxy for income, which correlates with race. That's social scoring — and the liability sits with you, the deployer, not the model provider.

The compliance trap at scale: you can purchase a compliant model and remain non-compliant, because compliance is a property of the whole system, not the individual component.

The Stripping Argument

"We'll remove prohibited features from model inputs. No zip code, no employment gap, no proxy for a protected class."

This works for one model, one endpoint, one feature set, under today's regulatory interpretation. It fails at scale for three reasons.

Feature interactions are opaque. A gradient-boosted tree trained on 200 features might use zip code as a split criterion despite your intentions. You don't know until you run per-inference feature attribution — and that requires infrastructure that doesn't exist in your pipeline today.

Feature removal degrades performance. Published analyses show that removing location-based features from credit scoring models reduces AUC by 0.03-0.08 depending on the alternative feature set. At 15,000 applications/day, an 0.05 AUC drop translates to roughly 150 additional false rejections daily — each a potential discrimination complaint under existing law. You trade one regulatory exposure for another.

The regulation's definitions will evolve. The Commission can issue delegated acts clarifying what constitutes "social scoring." Your feature-removal approach is a snapshot of today's interpretation. Compliance infrastructure needs to be mutable without redeploying the model.

Instrument, Sample, Cap

The companies that handled February 2 without panic share one trait: compliance attestation in the inference pipeline before scale, not after. Three actions:

Instrument at the model-call boundary. Log every feature vector, model version, and routing decision where features enter the model — not at the application boundary, not in the API gateway. This is your audit trail. Without it, you're reconstructing decisions from logs designed for debugging, not compliance.

Run prohibited-practice checks as inference middleware. Full SHAP attribution on every call adds 50-200ms of latency. Instead, sample: run attribution on 2-5% of inference calls and flag any pathway where a prohibited feature's contribution exceeds a threshold you define. This turns a 5,625-analyst problem into an automated alerting system with bounded cost. Flagged pathways go to a human. Clean pathways produce a machine-attested record of non-violation.

Track compliance surfaces as a first-class architecture metric. Compute microservices × model endpoints weekly. Call it audit surface area. Cap it. Before adding a microservice, staff the attestation work for the new compliance surfaces it creates — before writing code. This makes the cost of architectural complexity visible in sprint planning, where it belongs.

The reason this catches leaders off guard: compliance costs are non-linear. They stay near-zero through pilot and early production, then spike when volume crosses the threshold where manual review becomes impossible. Your brain extrapolates the early flat curve forward and underestimates the spike. The fix isn't better intuition — it's making the cost function explicit before you scale.

Count your production model-call endpoints. Multiply by the number of microservices that call them. That's your audit surface area. If you can't tell a regulator — today — which of those surfaces use a feature that could constitute social scoring under Article 5, you've already failed the February 2 deadline. Not because you're non-compliant. Because you can't demonstrate compliance, and in this regulation, the inability to demonstrate is the violation.
