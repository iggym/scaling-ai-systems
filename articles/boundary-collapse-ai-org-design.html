When Code Replaces Departments

On February 27, 2024, Klarna CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski posted a chart that broke the traditional customer service model. An OpenAI-powered assistant had handled 2.3 million customer conversations in a single month. The software did the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents.

BEAT 1

BEFORE: Engineering teams build tools for humans to use.
AFTER: Engineering teams build digital employees with their own P&L.
TURN: The moment an AI agent handles 2.3 million tasks, it stops being an IT asset and becomes an operational unit.

The Boundary Collapse

Scaling AI isn't a model problem; it's a boundary problem. When an agent's output volume rivals a human department, the wall between platform engineering, ML operations, and business operations collapses. You cannot manage a 700-agent equivalent using an IT ticketing system.

The cost of an AI agent doesn't scale linearly with usage; it scales with the complexity of the context window. Your biggest bill comes from your hardest cases, not your most frequent ones.

Cost Anatomy

Look at the numbers. Replacing 700 human agents at a fully loaded cost of $40,000 each runs about $28 million a year. Running 2.3 million conversations through the OpenAI API—assuming 2,000 input and 1,000 output tokens per chat at GPT-4 pricing—costs roughly $276,000 a month. That is $3.3 million a year. The math yields a $24.7 million annual savings, aligning tightly with Klarna's claimed $40 million profit impact.

The strongest counterargument is that AI agents degrade service quality and push complexity onto customers. Klarna reported customer satisfaction scores equal to human agents, with average resolution times dropping from 11 minutes to under 2 minutes. The nuance lies in the shifted cost-quality tradeoff curve. Keeping humans on tier-1 queries became an unjustifiable capital expense.

Mental Model: Tool

IT owns the software. Operations owns the humans. The software assists the humans.

Mental Model: Employee

Ops owns the agent's success metrics. Platform owns the agent's infrastructure. The agent replaces the humans.

The brain predicts outcomes based on past models. When an agent handles millions of tasks, our mental model of "tool" breaks, forcing a prediction error that restructures how we assign accountability. We stop seeing a script and start seeing a colleague.

BEAT 2

BEFORE: We need a centralized AI team to build our agents.
AFTER: We need platform infrastructure owned by one group, and agent design owned by product teams.
TURN: Two days after Klarna's announcement, GitHub launched Copilot Enterprise for $39 per user per month. The pricing forced a structural admission: a central AI team cannot manage custom code indexing, compliance, and RAG pipelines for every department.

The Infra-Product Split

If you are an engineering leader past MVP, you need to restructure how AI ownership works right now. Split the AI org. Platform engineering owns the model gateways, token budget, and RAG infrastructure. Product teams own the prompts, tool integrations, and agent success metrics. Assign a human P&L owner to every agent. If an agent does the work of 700 people, someone needs to be accountable for its output, just like a department head. Budget by task complexity, not volume. Track the token cost of your hardest 1% of queries, because that is where your margin bleeds.

Audit your highest-volume automated workflow today. Find the human manager who would be accountable if that workflow were a team of people, and assign them ownership of the agent's success metrics.
