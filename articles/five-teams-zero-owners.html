Five Teams, Zero Owners

March 14, 2025

Your org chart assumes one owner per system. Agents have five.

In February 2025, Sebastian Siemiatkowski posted that Klarna's AI assistant was handling 2.3 million conversations a month—work equivalent to 700 full-time agents. The number that should have scared him wasn't the 700. It was the five.

Five distinct internal teams who all had a legitimate claim to own that system. Not one of them could fix it end-to-end when something went wrong. Customer service operated it but didn't build it. Engineering owned the integration layer; the conversation flows belonged to no one. Compliance held veto power over prompt changes without the ability to author them. Product set the roadmap but had no control over model behavior. And data fed the training pipeline—while prompt logic sat in a gap between all five.

If the AI told a customer their refund was processed when it wasn't, which team filed the incident? Customer service saw the complaint first. Engineering could reproduce the model output. Compliance had a regulatory concern about incorrect financial information. Product owned the feature that triggered the interaction. Data might have caused it through a pipeline change the week before. Five teams. One bug. No single owner with enough context to triage it without a cross-team meeting.

Five teams. One bug. No single owner with enough context to triage it without a cross-team meeting.

Klarna isn't an anomaly. It's a preview of the problem that hits every organization once their AI system crosses from pilot into real traffic.

Your org chart assumes one owner per system. Agents have five.

That's the fracture. The organizational structure wrapped around a system that doesn't fit inside any single box on the chart.

BEFORE: Every system in your stack has a clear owning team. That's how you've shipped software for a decade.
AFTER: Your AI system has five teams with legitimate claims and no written contract between any of them.
TURN: Ownership is the wrong abstraction. Interfaces are the right one.

An AI agent handling customer conversations lives at the intersection of five organizational domains simultaneously. Your org chart has one box for each domain and no box for the intersection. When a system's span of concerns exceeds any single team's mandate, the organization doesn't fail to find an owner—it finds too many, and none of them is wrong about having a claim.

Microsoft hit a different edge of the same problem in March 2024. The company created a "Microsoft AI" division under Mustafa Suleyman, absorbing core talent from Inflection AI—Karen Simonyan as chief scientist, plus key researchers and engineers from the Inflection arrangement. The stated intent: a dedicated organization for AI with crisp ownership boundaries. The structural effect: a new horizontal layer inserted between established product groups—Microsoft 365, GitHub, Dynamics—and the model capabilities those groups depended on.

A Microsoft 365 team wanting to ship a new Copilot feature faced a constraint that didn't exist six months earlier. The product team owned the user-facing experience and the customer use case. Microsoft AI owned the model integration and the inference behavior. Both had to align on scope, timeline, and what "done" meant. If the feature underperformed in production—say, the Copilot response drifted off-topic in a specific scenario—was that a product design issue or a model behavior issue? The product team could argue the prompt template needed adjustment. Microsoft AI could argue the model routing was sound and the product team's context window was misconfigured. Both positions had merit. Neither team could unilaterally diagnose, fix, or ship the resolution.

That's the layered ownership conflict. A horizontal AI layer doesn't clarify who owns what. It creates ambiguous ownership at every integration point because both the layer and the product team hold a valid claim—and operate under different roadmaps, different success metrics, and different incentive structures.

The strongest counterargument is that a centralized AI team prevents inconsistent model usage and duplicated engineering across product groups. That cost—building the same RAG pipeline twice, using different models with different safety profiles—is real and visible in an architecture review. Klarna's case demonstrates the opposite failure mode. Centralization creates a single coordination bottleneck. Teams closest to a customer problem can't iterate without routing through it. The cost of inconsistency appears in a design review. The cost of coordination appears in cycle time—which you notice three months later, in a missed roadmap commit, with no clear line item to blame.

(Every organization I've worked with on this problem spends three to six months in the reorganization before someone points out that the org chart wasn't the variable that mattered. That's not neuroscience—that's written-in-retros terms, and it repeats.)

The model got cheaper. The organization got more expensive.

BEFORE: Adding a dedicated AI team gives us clear ownership and faster iteration.
AFTER: Adding a dedicated AI team gave us two owners for every feature and zero owners for every production incident.
TURN: More owners doesn't mean clearer ownership. It means more handoffs, and handoffs are where scale breaks.

Intercom's trajectory makes the oscillation visible at the company level. CEO Eoghan McCabe reorganized around the company's AI agent, Fin—first centralizing AI specialists into a dedicated team, then distributing them into product groups when the centralized model became a shipping bottleneck, then confronting the consistency problems that distribution created across products. Centralize, distribute, re-centralize. Each swing feels like progress. None of them address the underlying issue: they're all treating AI ownership as a team-placement question when it's an interface-contract question.

The cost anatomy at Klarna's scale is blunt. Seven hundred FTE-equivalent agents at a blended global customer-service cost basis of roughly $35,000 to $43,000 per year represent $24.5 million to $30.1 million in annual labor cost. The AI system handling that volume costs a fraction in compute and API calls. But the organizational cost of coordinating five teams around every prompt change, every model update, every compliance review is invisible on a P&L. It shows up somewhere else. A change that took two to three days when one team owned the full stack now takes two to three weeks when five teams share it. That 5–7x slowdown, multiplied across every iteration every quarter, means the coordination tax erodes the cost advantage the AI was purchased to deliver. The model got cheaper. The organization got more expensive—specifically, the engineering hours burned in cross-team alignment, the sprint cycles lost waiting on another team's review, the on-call rotations that couldn't diagnose an issue outside their layer.

More owners doesn't mean clearer ownership. It means more handoffs, and handoffs are where scale breaks.

Find the inflection yourself

At 3 teams, coordination stays below compute for all iterations shown.

Map the concern span

For every AI system in production, list each team that can affect its behavior or is affected by its output. If you exceed two names without a written contract between them, you've found the fracture. At Klarna, the list was five. At most organizations past MVP, it's three to four—and growing.

Write interface contracts

Not "Team X owns the AI system." Instead: "Team X controls prompt logic and is accountable for p95 latency under 200ms. Team Y controls training data and is accountable for accuracy above 94%. Team Z holds compliance veto and commits to 48-hour response on any flagged change." Contracts are verifiable in a sprint review. Ownership assignments aren't verifiable at all.

Build incident protocols

When the AI produces a wrong answer at 2 AM, who gets paged, in what sequence, with what authority to roll back or suppress? If answering this takes more than 30 seconds, the problem isn't ownership—it's an unspecified coordination flow, and no reorg fixes what hasn't been specified.

Measure coordination tax

Count the cross-team handoffs required to ship one AI feature change from proposal to production. Above three, the org structure costs more than the technology it supports. Track this number monthly. It stays flat (the contracts are working) or it climbs (the reorg added layers without adding clarity).

Within 24 hours: pick the AI system closest to production in your organization. List every team that touches it. If you pass two names without a written interface contract between them, you've found the fracture. Write the first contract before the week ends.
