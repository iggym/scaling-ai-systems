Own the Front Door

What Klarna and Microsoft discovered when their AI teams became the bottleneck they were supposed to eliminate.

Mar 14, 2025 Org Design Case Study
The reframe at a glance
Center of Excellence
Shipping Bottleneck

In February 2025, Klarna's AI assistant handled 2.3 million customer service conversations in a single month. That volume represents the work of roughly 700 full-time agents — and it flows through a system built by a team that doesn't report to customer service, doesn't sit inside the product organization, and can't be iterated on by either without joining a centralized queue.

Sebastian Siemiatkowski, Klarna's CEO, described the shift as forcing a fundamental rethink of organizational ownership. The AI system wasn't a tool the customer service team used. It was the customer service team. And the people authorized to change its behavior didn't work for the department whose outcomes depended on it.

2.3 million conversations per month — 700 agents' worth of work — flowing through a team that doesn't report to the department whose outcomes depend on it.

Post Share
Perspective Shift
BEFORE: We need a stronger, better-resourced AI platform team to keep up with demand.
AFTER: The platform team is the bottleneck. Adding capacity makes the bottleneck wider, not shorter.
TURN: The thing you're scaling to fix the problem is the problem.

What looks like a reporting-lines problem with AI at scale is actually an iteration-loop problem. When your AI system becomes the primary interface between your company and your customers, the team that owns it becomes either the fastest or the slowest loop in your organization. Centralize that loop, and you've built a brand-new bottleneck dressed up as a center of excellence.

Every hour a product manager spends waiting for the AI team's capacity is an hour your competitor's product manager spends shipping.

Money in the Handoffs

Here's the anatomy of why this breaks, using Klarna's numbers. At 2.3 million conversations per month, even if just 0.1% surface a behavior needing adjustment — a misrouted query, a tone problem, a missing edge case — that's 2,300 adjustments per month. A centralized AI team of eight engineers handles roughly 288 each, about ten per working day.

Each one requires context from the customer service side. Not a Jira ticket. Actual domain understanding — why this query misrouted, what the customer meant, what the correct resolution should have been. Two hours per adjustment, conservatively. That's 4,600 hours of context transfer per month against 1,280 available working hours. The math doesn't resolve.

Adding headcount doesn't fix it. Each new engineer adds iteration capacity but also their own context-transfer overhead — they need to learn the product domain before they can act. A team of sixteen might increase output by 30% while doubling AI engineering compensation. The marginal return on centralized headcount goes negative fast. The failure mode doesn't look like a budget problem. It looks like a shipping-delay problem.

4,600 hours of context transfer needed per month. 1,280 hours available. The math doesn't resolve — and no headcount fix changes the shape of that gap.

Redrawing the Line

Microsoft hit a structurally identical wall in March 2025. Copilot integrations across Office, Windows, and GitHub all depended on a centralized AI platform team for behavior-level changes. Product teams queued. Ship dates slipped not because the model lacked capability, but because the team authorized to tune its behavior for a specific product surface couldn't serve three surfaces demanding changes at once.

The reorganization moved dedicated AI engineers into each product group. The central platform narrowed to shared infrastructure: model serving, evaluation frameworks, deployment automation. Product-level prompt tuning and behavior adjustment moved into the product orgs.

Microsoft didn't add headcount. They redistributed existing headcount to change where the iteration loop lived. The signal: the problem wasn't insufficient AI engineers. It was AI engineers in the wrong room.

Perspective Shift
BEFORE: Product teams don't understand the AI well enough to own it — they'll break things.
AFTER: AI teams don't understand the product well enough to iterate on it — they're already breaking things, just slowly.
TURN: The knowledge gap runs both directions, and only one side holds the context that determines whether a change helps the user.

Guarding the Rails

The strongest case for centralized AI ownership is safety and consistency. Distribute behavior ownership across product teams and you get drift — one surface chatty, another terse, nobody holding a holistic view of what the AI promises customers.

That case holds at one surface. At three or more, the centralized team's capacity to enforce consistency degrades faster than the distributed model's tendency to create inconsistency. You trade a theoretical risk for an actual shipping failure. The answer is centralized standards and tooling with distributed behavior ownership — a platform team that builds guardrails and evaluation infrastructure, not one that approves every prompt change.

A brief aside: cognitive load research documents a measurable cost in error rate and decision speed when people context-switch between domains. When your org structure forces someone to hold product requirements and model behavior in working memory simultaneously, they'll be slower and less accurate at both than someone embedded in one domain. AI makes this cost visible because the iteration volume turns accumulated slowness into a line item.

Floor Gives Out

The pattern across these cases points to a specific break point, and it isn't headcount. It's surface count. The centralized AI team model holds at one product surface. It strains at two. It fails at three or more — not from capacity constraints, but because domain context can't transfer through a ticket queue at the speed AI systems demand. The platform team fails from an operating model that assumes context is portable. At these volumes, it isn't.

Four Structural Moves

  1. Count your surfaces. Map every customer- or revenue-touching surface where AI behavior is directly visible. Search results, recommendations, generated content, conversational interfaces — each counts. If the number hits three or more, the centralized model is already failing. You haven't measured it yet.
  2. Move the loop, not the people. Ensure the person who notices an AI behavior problem can fix it, or is at most one handoff away. Count handoffs between "problem noticed" and "fix deployed." More than two means restructure.
  3. Shrink the platform mandate. Model serving, evaluation tooling, prompt versioning, observability — platform concerns. Product-level prompt tuning, behavior adjustment, feature-specific AI logic — not platform concerns. If your platform team does product-level work, that's the smell. Write the boundary down.
  4. Track iteration latency, not headcount. The leading indicator: time from "behavior problem identified" to "fix in production," measured per product surface. If one surface is consistently slower, the structure is wrong for that surface regardless of what the org chart says.

Within the next 24 hours, count your AI-touching product surfaces. Write the number down. If it's three or more, you have a structural bottleneck that no platform-team hiring will fix. The restructure isn't optional. You just get to choose the timing — or let the next shipping delay choose it for you.

Key terms in this piece Iteration loop — the cycle from noticing an AI behavior problem to deploying a fix.

Surface count — the number of distinct customer-touching surfaces where AI behavior is visible.

Context-transfer tax — the hours spent moving domain knowledge into the team authorized to act on it.
