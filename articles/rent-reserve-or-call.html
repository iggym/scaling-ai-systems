Rent, Reserve, or Call

Self-hosting looks cheap per GPU-hour. It is only cheap for the hours you keep the GPU busy — and on-demand pricing loses that bet before you start.

2026-10-075 min readinference-infrastructure · cost-economics · gpu-capacity · vendor-lock-in
Common Assumption

At our volume, running our own open model on cloud GPUs will be cheaper than paying per token.

Actual Inflection

Cost per token is GPU price ÷ (throughput × utilization). On-demand H100s at an assumed 1,500 tok/s cost $1.27/M even at 100% busy; the commitment term matters more than the serving engine.

Before scale

In June 2025 AWS cut on-demand prices for its H100-based P5 instances by 44%, with savings-plan reductions of up to 45%.[1][2] After the cut, a p5.48xlarge — eight H100 GPUs — lists at about $55.04 an hour on demand in us-east-1, or roughly $6.88 per GPU-hour.[3]

Price cuts like this restart a familiar conversation in engineering leadership: GPUs are cheaper, open-weight models are good enough, so stop paying a markup per token and run inference yourselves. The spreadsheet usually multiplies a GPU-hour price by the hours in a month and divides by a throughput figure from a benchmark.

That spreadsheet is missing the variable that decides the outcome. A GPU bills for every hour it exists. It only produces tokens in the hours you have requests to give it.

Perspective shift — beat 1
“Self-hosting is cheaper per token”→“Self-hosting is cheaper per busy hour”

An API bills for tokens served; idle time is the provider's problem. A GPU reservation bills for time; idle time is yours. Traffic for most products follows a daily cycle with a peak several times the trough, and capacity has to cover the peak. So average utilization of self-hosted inference sits well below 100% even in a well-run fleet — and every point of idle time is a direct markup on the cost of the tokens you do serve.

Utilization Is the Unit Price

The cost of a self-hosted token is (GPU price per hour × GPUs) ÷ (tokens per second × 3,600 × utilization). Teams spend weeks on the throughput term — engines, quantisation, batching — and minutes on utilization. But utilization moves the result just as much, and the commitment term moves the GPU price by close to half.

Throughput figures are also less certain than spreadsheets suggest. Cerebrium's benchmark of Llama 3.1 70B in FP8 on a single H100 reported a best throughput of about 460 tokens per second at batch size 64, with results varying across vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.[4] Other configurations, sequence lengths and batch sizes produce figures several times higher or lower. Treat any single benchmark as one point on a wide range, and measure on your own traffic shape before you commit.

So the decision is not “is self-hosting cheaper?” It is “can we keep enough GPUs busy enough, for long enough, at a price we've locked in?” That is a bet on forecast demand, made with a multi-year commitment.

After scale
Perspective shift — beat 2
“We'll commit for three years to get the good price”→“We just made a three-year demand forecast”

The reserved price only exists in exchange for a commitment, and the commitment converts a cost question into a forecasting question. If demand grows faster than planned, you buy on-demand capacity for the overflow at the expensive rate. If it grows slower — or a better model arrives that runs on half the hardware, or an API price cut lands — the reservation keeps billing for idle GPUs. Token prices have fallen fast and unevenly. A three-year reservation is a bet that this year's trade-off still holds in year three, made at the point when you know least about year three.

What the Decision Usually Hinges On

Run the numbers for your own workload and a pattern usually appears: the inputs that look technical are secondary, and the ones that look like finance decide the answer.

Utilization is set by the shape of demand, not the engine. A product with a daily peak three times its trough averages well under 50% if capacity is sized for the peak, unless you can fill the troughs with deferrable work such as batch scoring, evaluation runs or embedding refreshes. That is why the strongest self-hosting cases tend to combine steady, high-volume traffic with a queue of flexible work to soak up idle hours.

The reasons that survive the arithmetic are often not about cost at all: control over model versions, data that cannot leave your infrastructure, fine-tuned weights a hosted API won't serve, or latency only co-location provides. Those are legitimate. They should be named as the reason, with the cost premium written next to them, rather than hidden inside an optimistic utilization assumption.

Your Counterargument

“Your throughput assumption is conservative. With a tuned engine, speculative decoding and FP8 we get far more than 1,500 tokens per second per GPU, and then self-hosting wins easily — even on demand.”

It might. Higher throughput lowers the curve in direct proportion: double it and on-demand break-even falls from 127% to about 64%. Use the calculator above with your measured number. But measure it on production-shaped traffic — your real prompt lengths, output lengths, concurrency and latency targets — not a benchmark sized for peak throughput. Latency targets cap batch size, and batch size is where most benchmark throughput comes from.[4]

Then add the costs the formula leaves out: the engineers who keep the fleet patched, scaled and on call, headroom for failover, and the cost of evaluating and migrating whenever a better open model ships. At an assumed $250K per engineer and a $0.30 saving per million tokens, it takes roughly 26,000 tokens per second, sustained, just to pay for one person.

What to Do

  1. Put utilization in the business case — measured, not assumed.

    Take a month of production traffic, size capacity for its peak, and compute average utilization at that size. Use that number in the cost-per-token formula, not 100%.

    This week: pull hourly token volume for your largest workload for the past 30 days.

  2. Price the commitment term explicitly.

    Show on-demand, 1-year and 3-year costs side by side with the break-even utilization for each. Make finance and engineering sign the same demand forecast.

    This week: get current quotes for all three terms in your target region.

  3. Fill the troughs before buying the peak.

    List deferrable workloads — batch scoring, evaluation runs, embedding refreshes — that can run in off-peak hours on the same fleet. They are the cheapest way to raise utilization.

    This week: list three deferrable jobs and their nightly token volume.

  4. Keep the API path warm.

    Route a share of traffic through a hosted API even after self-hosting, with the same prompts and evals. It is your overflow capacity, your price benchmark and your exit if the reservation turns out wrong.

    This week: confirm your gateway can switch a workload between self-hosted and API with configuration alone.

In the next 24 hours

Open the self-hosting business case on your desk and find its utilization assumption. If it is above 60% and wasn't measured from your own traffic, re-run the numbers with your real daily curve before anyone signs a commitment.
