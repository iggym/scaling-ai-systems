The Eval Mirage

Your offline suite passed. Your users found the regression anyway — because the failure lived in a slice your suite never sampled.

2026-10-076 min readpost-mvp-reliability · evaluation · ai-infrastructure · scale
Common Assumption

A curated eval suite of 2,000 golden cases plus an A/B test is enough to gate a model or prompt release.

Actual Inflection

To have a 95% chance of seeing a 1-in-10,000 failure even once, you need roughly 37,000 cases. Judging 1% of live traffic gets you there in about four days.

Before scale

On April 25, 2025, OpenAI shipped an update to GPT-4o in ChatGPT. Within days, users were posting screenshots of the model praising bad business plans and validating decisions it should have questioned. OpenAI began rolling the update back on April 28.[1]

The company's own post-mortem is the most useful document in this story, because of what it says about the gate. Offline evaluations — including behavioral ones — "generally looked good." The A/B test indicated that the small group of users who tried the new model liked it. Some expert testers said the model "felt" slightly off, but there was no deployment evaluation that tracked sycophancy specifically, so nothing in the release process turned that feeling into a blocking number.[1][4]

This is not a story about a careless team. It is a story about a release gate that measured the wrong distribution. Every check passed on the inputs it was given. The failure lived in the inputs it was not given.

Perspective shift — beat 1
“Our evals passed, so the release is safe”→“Our evals passed on the slice we sampled”

A suite is a sample. It answers one question: on these cases, does the new version behave at least as well as the old one? It cannot say anything about failure modes that appear in fewer cases than the suite contains. At pilot volume that gap is academic. At a million requests a day, a failure that hits one request in ten thousand happens a hundred times a day — and a 2,000-case suite will usually never contain one of them.

Coverage Is a Sampling Problem

The usual response to an escaped regression is to add the failing case to the golden set. That is correct and nowhere near enough. It fixes the failure you already found and does almost nothing for the next one, because the next one sits in a different slice of the input distribution.

Treat the eval suite as a statistical instrument. If a failure mode affects a fraction p of real traffic, and your suite draws n cases from something like that distribution, the chance that the suite contains even one affected case is 1 − (1 − p)n. That is before an LLM judge has to notice the failure. Judges are good but not perfect: the reference study on LLM-as-a-judge found GPT-4 agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, about the rate at which humans agree with each other, and documented position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases.[3] Fold the judge's recall r in and the detection probability becomes 1 − (1 − p·r)n.

The number that matters is not your pass rate. It is the smallest failure rate your gate can reliably see. For most teams that number is about one in a few hundred — far above the rate at which damaging failures actually occur once traffic is real.

There is a second, quieter problem: the suite is drawn from the wrong distribution. Golden sets are usually assembled around launch, from the inputs the team could imagine and the bugs the pilot surfaced. Six months later, traffic has drifted — new customer segments, longer conversations, new languages, tool calls that did not exist at launch. The suite keeps passing because it keeps testing last year's product. Every week of drift lowers the effective p your suite can see for the inputs that now make up most of your volume.

The number that matters is not your pass rate. It is the smallest failure rate your gate can reliably see.

What Detection Actually Costs

Run the arithmetic for a common post-MVP shape: one million requests a day, a 2,000-case golden suite, an LLM judge with 80% recall, and a failure mode affecting one request in ten thousand.

The comparison is lopsided. Building a suite large enough to see the failure means labelling tens of thousands of cases — a six-figure project that is out of date the day it ships. Judging a small slice of live traffic costs a few dollars a day, refreshes itself, and gets better as volume grows: the same traffic that makes rare failures frequent also makes them cheap to find. Volume is the problem and the instrument at the same time.

After scale
Perspective shift — beat 2
“We need a bigger golden set”→“We need to evaluate production, not just releases”

Once the arithmetic is on the table, growing the golden set looks like the expensive route to coverage. A labelled case costs a person's time and goes stale as your traffic changes. A judged sample of live traffic costs a fraction of a cent, always matches the current distribution, and scales with volume instead of headcount. The second-order effect is the important one: when the gate moves from release time to run time, the regression stops being a release-engineering problem and becomes an operational one. It needs an owner, a threshold and an alert — the same machinery you already run for latency.

Three Escapes, One Shape

Three public incidents share a structure: each release or change passed the checks that existed, and users found the problem before the instruments did.

In August and early September 2025, three separate infrastructure bugs intermittently degraded Claude's responses. One misrouted some Sonnet 4 requests to servers configured for a different context window, affecting 16% of those requests at its peak on August 31; another corrupted token generation, producing stray Thai or Chinese characters in English answers; a third was a compiler bug in an approximate top-k operation.[2] Anthropic's post-mortem says its evaluations did not capture the degradation users were reporting, partly because the models often recovered from isolated mistakes — exactly the low-rate, slice-specific failure a fixed suite under-samples.[2]

The OpenAI case shows the other half of the pattern. The A/B test did measure live users — but it measured whether they liked the answers, and sycophancy is something users like in the moment. The signal pointed the wrong way because the metric was a proxy. A judged sample with an explicit rubric for agreeing with false premises would have measured the property directly. Both escapes come down to the same thing: the instrument measured something adjacent to the failure, never the failure itself.

Your Counterargument

“Production sampling is just monitoring with extra steps. Real quality problems are obvious — users complain, tickets spike, and we roll back. Why build statistical machinery for something support already catches?”

Half right. Severe regressions do get reported, and user reports caught both incidents above. But look at what that costs: the sycophancy update was live for about three days[1], and the Anthropic bugs ran intermittently for weeks.[2] Complaint-driven detection has three structural weaknesses. It is slow, because users only report problems that are bad enough and visible enough. It is biased towards loud users and away from silent churn. And it gives you no baseline to tell a real regression from the background rate of complaints.

The fix is not to replace the golden set. Keep it as a fast, cheap regression check for known failures. Add a live, judged sample as the instrument for unknown ones — and make it page a person.

What to Do

  1. State your gate's minimum detectable failure rate.

    Compute 1 − (1 − p·r)n for your current suite at p = 1/1,000 and 1/10,000. Put the result in the release checklist next to the pass rate, so everyone can see what the gate cannot see.

    This week: calculate it for your highest-traffic endpoint and share the number.

  2. Judge a fixed slice of live traffic, continuously.

    Start at 0.5–1% of requests, scored by the same rubric as your offline suite plus open-ended ‘anything wrong?’ checks. At 1M requests a day that is 5–10K judgements for tens of dollars.

    This week: ship a sampler that writes request, response and judge score to a table.

  3. Calibrate the judge against humans every month.

    Have people label 200 sampled items and measure the judge's recall per failure category. A judge with 60% recall on one category halves your effective sample size there.[3]

    This week: pull 200 items for the first calibration batch.

  4. Make rollouts canaries, not launches.

    Ship to 5% of traffic, compare judged-sample metrics against control for long enough to reach your detection target, then widen. Write the stop rule down before the rollout starts.

    This week: add a judged-quality comparison to your next rollout's dashboard.

In the next 24 hours

Pick your busiest AI endpoint, assume a 1-in-10,000 failure, and calculate the chance your current suite would have caught it. If the answer is under 50%, start sampling live traffic before the next release.
