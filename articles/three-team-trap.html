The Three-Team Trap

When your single AI owner becomes the bottleneck — and the reorg costs more than the model.

Common Assumption

You need 10 AI-touching teams before a platform team pays for itself. Until then, let each team own their stack.

Actual Inflection

The crossover hits at 3.7 teams. By four teams, the platform model is already cheaper. The duplicated cost is running right now.

Before scale Klarna's AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in a single month. Work that previously required 700 full-time agents. By early 2025, headcount had dropped from roughly 5,000 to 3,000. On paper, a machine-learning fairy tale.

Inside the org, a different signal. The 15-person AI team serving five product verticals had a six-week backlog for any new model integration. Product teams stopped waiting. They built their own — separate API keys, separate prompt libraries, separate evaluation rigs, separate guardrail configs. Five verticals. Five independent AI stacks. Zero shared infrastructure. Each stack cost something. Nobody was counting the total.

Perspective Shift [BEAT 1]

"We need more AI engineers" → "We need fewer people touching models directly"

The bottleneck wasn't headcount. It was access. One team owning all model decisions created a single throttle that five product teams competed for simultaneously. Adding engineers to that team wouldn't clear the queue — the queue existed because the org structure required every AI decision to route through one group, not because the group was small. More people at the bottleneck doesn't widen the pipe. It adds more people standing at the same pipe.

Platform Inflection

The platform-team inflection doesn't arrive when you have too many AI projects. It arrives when duplicated AI infrastructure across teams costs more than a platform team would — and that crossover happens at three AI-touching product teams, not the ten most leaders assume.

The same org chart that gave you speed at two teams generates invisible cost at four.

Where Money Goes

Reconstruct the per-team annual cost of running an independent AI stack. Real numbers, from the tooling market in early 2025:

  • Prompt management and versioning: $15,000
  • Evaluation pipeline (automated + human review): $20,000
  • LLM observability and tracing: $15,000
  • Guardrails and compliance layer: $10,000
  • Engineer time on AI infra maintenance (0.25 FTE @ $160K): $40,000

One team, one stack: roughly $100,000 per year. Reasonable. Manageable. Nobody flags it.

Now run five teams with independent stacks. Same $100K each. $500K total. Still looks fine — each budget owner sees their own $100K and approves it without escalation.

A platform team that builds this infrastructure once: two platform engineers at $160K each ($320K), plus $50K in shared tooling licenses. Total: $370,000 per year. Serves all five teams.

The break-even: $370K ÷ $100K = 3.7 teams. At four AI-touching product teams, the platform team is already cheaper.

At five, you're burning $130K per year in pure duplication — money that buys nothing, ships nothing, and accelerates nothing. At ten teams, $630K. At Klarna's five verticals, the duplicated infra alone was likely $130K annually, on top of the six-week queue cost measured in delayed shipments and lost revenue.

Perspective Shift [BEAT 2]

"Each team owns their AI integration" → "Each team owns their integration AND the duplicated infra nobody can see"

None of this appears in any single budget line. It's distributed across five team budgets, each of which looks reasonable in isolation. Nobody sees the total because nobody owns the total. This is the structural trap: the cost of decentralization scales linearly with the number of teams, but each team's slice looks small enough to approve without a second thought. The budget system is built to hide this.

Mandate Accelerant

After scale Shopify hit the same pattern from a different angle. In February 2025, CEO Tobi Lütke sent an internal memo requiring teams to prove they'd tried AI before requesting new headcount. The intent: force adoption across the org. The org consequence: without a platform team providing shared AI infrastructure, product teams built their own integrations in isolation. Different models for similar tasks. No shared evaluation harness. No unified cost tracking. No cross-team learning about what prompts actually worked.

Lütke called it "AI-first." Inside the engineering org, it was shadow AI — functional, ungoverned, each instance invisible to anyone outside the team that built it. Each team's integration worked well enough to satisfy the mandate. None of them worked well enough to be maintainable at scale.

The mandate accelerated usage. It also accelerated fragmentation. At this org scale, those are the same event. Every team that spins up its own LLM integration creates a new cost center that nobody is tracking centrally, and a new failure surface that nobody is monitoring centrally. The mandate worked. The structure didn't.

Your Counterargument

"Centralized platform teams slow teams down. I've seen this — a platform team that becomes a gatekeeper, and now product teams wait three months for API access. Aren't we just recreating the bottleneck?"

This is the strongest objection, and it's half-right. The platform team as gatekeeper reproduces the original problem with different branding. You've swapped "the AI team" for "the platform team" and kept the queue.

The model that works is the platform team as internal vendor, not approval layer. They own the shared infrastructure — model routing, cost allocation, evaluation pipelines, guardrails — but product teams own their prompts, their logic, their deployment cadence. The platform team provides the road. Product teams choose their speed and destination. Gatekeeping is a failure mode of the platform-team pattern, not an inevitable property of it.

The distinction matters operationally. A gatekeeper platform team says: "You may use GPT-4 if we approve your use case." A vendor platform team says: "Here's the routing layer, here's the cost tracker, here's the evaluation harness — pick your model and ship." The first creates queues. The second eliminates duplicated work without creating any. If your platform team is generating approval tickets, you've built the wrong kind.

What to Do

1. Count your AI-touching teams today. Not "AI teams" — teams that have called an LLM API in the last 90 days. If that number is three or more, you're already past the cost inflection. The duplicated spend is running right now. The platform-team proposal should start this week, not next quarter. Every week of delay is $2,500+ in duplicated cost at five teams.

2. Build the shared layer first. Model routing, cost allocation, and evaluation are infrastructure — ship them like infrastructure, with SLAs and onboarding docs and a runbook, not policy documents and approval forms. Governance (model-access policies, compliance review) comes after teams have a reason to use the platform. If you lead with governance, teams will route around you, and you'll have a platform team with no users.

3. Budget 6–9 months of dual operation explicitly. During the reorg, both the old structure (teams building independently) and the new structure (platform team serving them) will run simultaneously. This is deadweight cost. It will feel like the reorg is making things more expensive before it makes them cheaper. That's because it is, temporarily. If you don't budget for this phase openly, you'll see the duplicate spend, panic, and abandon the reorg halfway through — leaving yourself with the worst of both structures and the credibility cost of a failed reorg.

The reason the 3.7-team inflection is so hard to see in real organizations is that each team's independent AI spend is categorized differently — one under "tooling," another under "cloud infrastructure," a third under "headcount." No single line item crosses the threshold that would trigger escalation. The budget format reinforces the structural trap.

24-Hour Action: Open your organization chart. Find every team that has called an LLM API in the last 90 days. Count them. If the number is three or more, draft the platform-team proposal before tomorrow's standup — not because it's urgent, but because the duplicated cost is already running and no single budget line will ever show it.
