Who Gets Paged for Wrong?

Uptime has a pager. Correctness has a backlog. At scale, the gap between them is measured in weeks of bad answers.

2026-10-075 min readorg-design · post-mvp-reliability · on-call · ai-agents
Common Assumption

Quality problems surface through support tickets and get fixed in the next sprint. On-call is for outages.

Actual Inflection

A quality incident affecting 5% of 500K daily requests costs $50K a day at $2 per bad answer. A quality rotation pays for itself if it cuts detection by three days, once a year.

Before scale

On August 5, 2025, a routing bug started sending a small share of Claude Sonnet 4 requests to servers configured for a different context window. A load-balancing change on August 29 made it worse: at the peak on August 31, 16% of Sonnet 4 requests were affected. Two more unrelated bugs landed in the same weeks — one corrupting token generation so English answers sprouted stray Thai or Chinese characters, another a compiler bug in the token-selection step.[1]

The systems stayed up. Latency, error rates and availability — the signals every on-call rotation is built around — showed nothing a pager would fire on. What users saw was a model that seemed, intermittently and hard to reproduce, a bit worse. Anthropic's post-mortem says its evaluations did not capture the degradation users were reporting, and that the overlapping bugs produced confusing, contradictory reports that made diagnosis harder.[1]

To its credit, Anthropic published a detailed technical account. But the shape of the incident is general. Any team running AI at scale has the same blind spot: the system is up and wrong at the same time, and nobody's pager is wired to "wrong".

Perspective shift — beat 1
“If it's broken, the on-call engineer will know”→“The on-call engineer will know if it's down”

Classic on-call is built on the four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation.[2] They work because in a deterministic service a broken path produces an error code. In an AI system the most expensive failure mode returns HTTP 200 with a fluent, confident, wrong answer. Every golden signal stays green. The failure exists only in the meaning of the output, and meaning is not a metric anyone is paged on.

Correctness Has No Owner

Ask who owns availability for your AI feature and you get one name. Ask who owns answer quality in production and you get a meeting: the product team owns the prompt, the platform team owns the gateway, the ML team owns the evals, the vendor owns the model, support owns the complaints. Each owns a cause. Nobody owns the symptom.

The Google SRE book draws exactly this line: good monitoring separates what is broken (the symptom) from why (the cause), and alerts on symptoms.[2] Most AI orgs have alerts only on causes — a deploy failed, a provider returned 5xx, a queue backed up. "Answers got worse" is a symptom with no instrument, no threshold and no rotation, so by default it is detected by the slowest sensor in the company: customers who complain, and support teams who notice a pattern.

Detection time is the variable that sets the bill. A quality incident's cost grows linearly with the days it runs, and without an owner, days are what it gets.

The Cost of Every Undetected Day

Take a mid-sized deployment: 500,000 AI-handled requests a day, a regression that degrades 5% of them, and a blended cost of $2 for every bad answer — a support contact, a refund, a re-run or a small share of churn. All three numbers are assumptions; replace them with yours.

The liability side is not hypothetical either. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (February 2024), a British Columbia tribunal held the airline responsible for a chatbot's wrong answer about bereavement fares, rejecting the argument that the bot was responsible for its own statements.[3][4] The damages were small — about C$650 — but the principle scales: every wrong answer is your answer.[3]

After scale
Perspective shift — beat 2
“Support will escalate quality issues”→“Support is our slowest, most expensive quality sensor”

When quality has no owner, support becomes the monitoring system by default — and it is a poor one. A support team sees individual complaints, not rates. It cannot tell a 5% regression from normal variation until the pattern is obvious, and by then the incident has run for weeks. Worse, the people who complain are a biased sample: the cost lands silently on users who simply stop trusting the feature. Routing quality detection through support also puts the most important AI signal in the part of the company with the least access to prompts, traces and model versions.

Up and Wrong at the Same Time

The public record has a consistent shape. In each case the service was up, the failure was in the content, and detection came from users rather than instruments.

OpenAI's sycophantic GPT-4o update in April 2025 is a cleaner example of the same gap: offline evaluations and an A/B test looked good, nothing in deployment tracked the behaviour that went wrong, and the update was rolled back about three days after it shipped.[5] Three days is fast — fast because the failure was visible and widely discussed. Most quality regressions are neither.

The same structure shows up in smaller deployments every week. A prompt edit that improves one intent and quietly degrades another. A retrieval index rebuilt with a broken chunker. A vendor model alias pointing to a new snapshot. A tool whose schema changed so the agent silently stops calling it. Each one returns 200 OK. Each one sits in the gap between the team that changed something and the team that would notice the effect.

Your Counterargument

“Quality isn't an on-call problem. You can't page someone at 3 a.m. because answers got 4% worse — there's nothing they could do at that hour, and it will burn the rotation out. Quality belongs in the sprint, not the pager.”

Partly right. Most quality signals should not wake anyone up, and a rotation that pages on noisy judge scores will be ignored within a month. But the conclusion does not follow. The goal is not 3 a.m. pages; it is a named person accountable for a quality SLO, a threshold that turns a trend into an incident, and a playbook that includes the cheapest fix available — rolling back a prompt, pinning a model snapshot, failing over to a fallback. Those actions take minutes and are reversible.

Business-hours ownership with clear severity levels works for most teams. Page only when the measured degradation crosses a level you have priced, like the one above. What doesn't work is no owner at all, which is the default today.

What to Do

  1. Write a quality SLO for each AI surface.

    Define it on a judged live sample — for example, “95% of sampled answers pass the rubric, measured over 24 hours” — next to the existing latency and availability SLOs.

    This week: draft the SLO for your highest-volume surface and get the product owner to sign it.

  2. Name one owner for the symptom.

    The product team that owns the user outcome should own the quality SLO. Platform supplies sampling and dashboards; the ML team supplies the rubric. Write this down — it is the decision most orgs never make.

    This week: put the owner's name in the service catalogue entry.

  3. Make rollback cheaper than investigation.

    Version prompts, pin model snapshots, and keep the previous configuration deployable with one command. The first response to a quality alert should take minutes, not a root-cause analysis.

    This week: confirm you can roll back the current prompt version in under five minutes.

  4. Route support signals into the same dashboard.

    Tag AI-related tickets automatically and plot their rate next to the judged-quality metric. Two weak signals that move together are a strong one.

    This week: add an ‘AI answer’ tag to your ticketing system.

In the next 24 hours

Ask, in writing, who is accountable if your AI feature's answers get 5% worse tomorrow. If the answer is a team, a meeting or silence, assign a person and give them a threshold before the week ends.
