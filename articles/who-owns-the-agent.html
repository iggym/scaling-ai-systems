Who Owns the Agent?

In early 2024, Klarna announced its OpenAI-powered assistant had handled 2.3 million customer conversations in a single month. The company claimed the system did the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents, projecting a $40 million reduction in support costs.

Engineering leaders globally copied the slide deck. The premise was simple: deploy the agent, retire the headcount.

Fourteen months later, the premise broke.

In May 2025, Reuters and Bloomberg reported Klarna was walking back its aggressive AI staffing cuts. Customer satisfaction had degraded. The AI could resolve isolated billing queries, but when a customer's issue spanned logistics, a missed payment, and a merchant dispute, the agent failed. The human agents who used to bridge those silos were gone. By the first quarter of 2026, Klarna booked a restructuring charge of roughly $200 million to rebuild the human-AI loop.

BEAT 1

The Orchestration Trap

This was not a model failure. It was an organizational boundary failure.

When an AI agent stays inside a single product pod, a centralized AI team can maintain it. When that agent must read state from the billing database, write to the logistics API, and trigger a retention email, it crosses organizational boundaries. The centralized "AI Platform" team does not own the billing database's schema. The logistics product team does not own the AI agent's context window.

Scaling AI is not a compute problem. It is an orchestration boundary problem.

In April 2025, Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke sent an internal memo that fundamentally altered how his engineering org approached scale. The mandate was blunt: before requesting human headcount, a team must prove AI cannot do the job. Managers were forced to ask what their area would look like if autonomous agents were already embedded in the team.

Shopify recognized that a centralized AI team becomes a bottleneck the moment an agent requires write-access to multiple product lines. If the supply chain pod needs the AI agent to reroute shipments, they cannot wait on a ticket to the central AI team to update the prompt.

BEAT 2

Rewiring Org Boundaries

Look at the cost anatomy of an agentic workflow in 2026. Industry data shows that 62 percent of agentic infrastructure costs do not come from model inference. The bill is driven by observability, state management, and the orchestration layer that ties disparate systems together. A centralized AI team can govern model routing. They cannot govern the business logic living in twelve different product microservices.

"When the orchestration layer accounts for 62 percent of the cost, the team owning the orchestration must own the P&L."

The counterargument is obvious: giving every product team autonomy over agents creates chaos, duplicated effort, and security nightmares. This is true for prompt engineering. It is false for state orchestration. The central AI team must own the guardrails, the model router, and the evaluation harness. The embedded product teams must own the agent's operational state and the specific API tools it can trigger.

The Boundary Shift

To survive the scale transition, engineering leaders must execute three structural shifts.

Action 1: The Silo Severance. Map your current human "bridge" roles. Do not automate their jobs with an agent until you have built a unified orchestration state layer that both teams can read and write to.

Action 2: The Orchestration Budget Shift. Move the line item for "Agent Observability" out of the centralized AI budget and into the product pods.

Action 3: The Write-Access Mandate. No agent is allowed in production unless the team owning its primary trigger event also owns the rollback mechanism.

Pull your agentic failure logs from the last seven days. Find the most frequent edge-case failure. Identify which product team's database triggered it, and check if that specific team's engineers have write-access to the agent's orchestration rules. Give them the keys today.
